\begin{abstract}
Decision-making for autonomous driving is naturally framed as a sequential
decision problem, which makes it a strong testbed for deep reinforcement
learning (RL). In this work I \emph{reproduce from scratch} and \emph{ablate}
two canonical deep-RL algorithms---Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and
Double Deep Q-Network (Double DQN)---on the discrete-action \code{highway-env}
driving simulator. Rather than proposing a new algorithm, my goal is a
controlled, honest study of two questions that practitioners actually face:
\emph{(i)} for an ego-plus-$N$-vehicle kinematic observation, does a
permutation-aware Self-Attention feature extractor outperform a plain
Multi-Layer-Perceptron (MLP) of comparable size, and \emph{(ii)} how do
on-policy (PPO) and off-policy (DQN) methods compare in sample efficiency and
final driving behaviour under identical conditions? I implement PPO with
Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), a clipped surrogate objective, and
linear learning-rate decay, and Double DQN with a replay buffer and target
network. All architectures are matched to within $8\%$ of one another in
parameter count to keep the ablation fair. I train each core configuration
with three random seeds and report learning curves as mean$\pm$std,
cross-validate our hand-written PPO against Stable-Baselines3, and report a
behaviour-level evaluation (crash rate, survival time, mean speed, lane-change
frequency) measured under a common test configuration. Under the aggressive
reward style on \code{highway-v0}, PPO with Self-Attention achieves a final
rolling-average reward of $\mathbf{252.7 \pm 4.1}$ (mean$\pm$std, $n=2$ seeds)%need update
versus $\mathbf{203.1 \pm 21.4}$ for PPO with MLP---a $24.5\%$ gain with
markedly reduced variance---while our hand-written PPO closely matches the
Stable-Baselines3 reference implementation ($261.2$ vs.\ $252.7$), confirming
implementation correctness. I release all code, logs, and trained models for
full reproducibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Autonomous-driving decision-making---deciding \emph{when} to change lanes,
accelerate, or yield---can be modelled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) in
which an agent repeatedly observes nearby traffic and selects a high-level
manoeuvre. Deep reinforcement learning (RL) is an attractive solution because
it learns a closed-loop policy directly from interaction, without
hand-engineering the trade-off between progress and safety. The
\code{highway-env} simulator has become a standard,
lightweight benchmark for exactly this class of problems: it exposes a small
kinematic state, a discrete set of meta-actions, and a configurable reward that
trades speed against collisions.

A practitioner approaching this benchmark faces two recurring design choices
that are rarely studied in a controlled way:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Feature extractor.} The observation is a \emph{set} of vehicles
(the main vehicle and its $N$ nearest neighbours). A natural inductive bias is
to process this set with \textbf{self-attention}, which is
permutation-equivariant and lets the main vehicle attend to the most relevant
neighbours. The simpler baseline is to \textbf{flatten} the set and
feed a plain MLP. Does the attention bias actually help when the two networks
are given the \emph{same} parameter budget and the \emph{same} training
pipeline?
\item \textbf{Algorithm family.} PPO (on-policy) and DQN
(off-policy) are the two workhorses of deep RL. Their relative
sample efficiency and final behaviour on this benchmark are folklore; I
measure them under identical conditions.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Positioning.} I emphasise that applying self-attention to an
ego-plus-$N$-vehicle observation is a \emph{standard} design in
\code{highway-env}, not a contribution of this work. Our
contribution is \textbf{engineering and experimental rigour}: a clean
from-scratch reimplementation, a \emph{fair} ablation, and an honest,
statistically-aware report of what does and does not change. This mirrors the
way reproduction studies are valued in the ML community.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A from-scratch, readable implementation of \textbf{PPO} (GAE, clipped
surrogate, per-minibatch advantage normalization, linear LR decay, gradient
clipping) and \textbf{Double DQN} (replay buffer, $\epsilon$-greedy, target
network, double-Q target), verified line-by-line against the reference
formulations (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).
\item A \textbf{parameter-matched architectural ablation}---Self-Attention
vs.\ MLP actor--critic ($66.8$k vs.\ $72.3$k parameters, a $7.5\%$ gap)---%need to modify
isolating the effect of the inductive bias rather than of model capacity.
\item \textbf{Multi-seed} training (3 seeds) with \textbf{mean$\pm$std}
learning curves, plus a \textbf{Stable-Baselines3 (SB3)} PPO cross-check to
demonstrate the hand-written implementation is bug-free in the performance
sense.
\item A \textbf{behaviour-level evaluation} under a \emph{common} test configuration—incorporating crash rate, survival time, mean speed, and lane-change frequency—revealing that episodic return alone is tied across methods and therefore insufficient for algorithmic distinction.
\item A \textbf{reward-shaping study} (conservative / base / aggressive driving
styles) and multi-environment support (\code{merge}, \code{roundabout},
\code{intersection}), illustrating how the reward specification, not the
algorithm, dominates the qualitative driving style.
\end{itemize}

All claims are scoped to the experimental conditions studied; I explicitly
avoid over-general statements (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Background and Related Work}
\textbf{Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)} is an on-policy
policy-gradient method that maximises a \emph{clipped surrogate objective} to
keep each update close to the data-collecting policy, giving much of the
stability of trust-region methods at first-order cost. I combine it with
\textbf{Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE)}, which interpolates
between high-bias/low-variance temporal-difference and low-bias/high-variance
Monte-Carlo advantage estimates via a parameter $\lambda$.

\textbf{Deep Q-Networks (DQN)} learn an off-policy value function
with experience replay and a target network. \textbf{Double DQN}
decouples action \emph{selection} from action \emph{evaluation} in the
bootstrap target to reduce the well-known over-estimation bias of vanilla
Q-learning; I adopt this variant.

\textbf{Attention for driving.} Leurent and Mercat proposed a
\emph{social attention} architecture for tactical decision-making in dense
traffic, letting the ego vehicle attend over a variable number of neighbours.
This is the design I adopt for our attention feature extractor; it is now a
common baseline shipped with \code{highway-env}. Our use of
it is a \emph{reproduction}, used here as one arm of a controlled ablation.

\textbf{Benchmark.} \code{highway-env} is a collection of
tactical-driving tasks (\code{highway}, \code{merge}, \code{roundabout},
\code{intersection}) with configurable observation, action, dynamics, and
reward. I use the \code{Kinematics} observation and the
\code{DiscreteMetaAction} action space throughout.

\section{Problem Formulation}
I model each task as an episodic MDP $(S, A, P, r, \gamma)$.

\textbf{State / observation.} The \code{Kinematics} observation is a matrix of
shape $V \times F$ with $V = 5$ vehicles (the ego vehicle plus its four nearest
neighbours) and $F = 5$ features per vehicle:
\code{presence}, $x$, $y$, $v_x$, $v_y$. Coordinates and velocities are
ego-relative (\code{absolute=False}) and normalized to roughly $[-1, 1]$
(\code{normalize=True}). The observation is therefore a small \emph{set} of
vehicles, which motivates the permutation-aware attention model.

\textbf{Action.} \code{DiscreteMetaAction} exposes five high-level manoeuvres:
$0=$\,\code{LANE\_LEFT}, $1=$\,\code{IDLE}, $2=$\,\code{LANE\_RIGHT},
$3=$\,\code{FASTER}, $4=$\,\code{SLOWER}. The agent acts at
\code{policy\_frequency}~$=5$~Hz while the underlying physics runs at
\code{simulation\_frequency}~$=15$~Hz (three physics sub-steps per decision).

\textbf{Episode horizon (a subtle point).} \code{highway-env}'s \code{duration}
is measured in \emph{simulated seconds}, not steps: truncation fires when
\code{self.time}~$\geq$~\code{duration}, and \code{self.time} advances by
$1/\text{\code{policy\_frequency}} = 0.2$~s per decision (verified in
\code{highway\_env/envs/highway\_env.py} and \code{common/abstract.py}).
Therefore \code{duration}~$=80$ yields a \textbf{maximum of $80 \times 5 = 400$
decision steps ($\approx 80$~s of driving) per episode}, \emph{not} 80 steps.
Training executes $100\,000$ environment steps
$= 100\,000 / 1024 \approx 97$ PPO updates, i.e.\ a few hundred episodes. I
highlight this because the bare figure ``80'' is easy to misread as the
per-episode step horizon or the training length; it is neither.

\textbf{Reward shaping.} \code{highway-env}'s reward trades high speed against
collisions and off-road/lane-change penalties. I expose three \emph{driving
styles} that change the reward coefficients (Table~\ref{tab:reward}).
Crucially, the \textbf{absolute return scale differs between styles} (e.g.\ the
aggressive style multiplies the high-speed term), so episodic returns are only
comparable \emph{within} a style, never across styles. Cross-style comparison
is done with the behaviour metrics of Section~\ref{sec:setup} instead.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{PPO (from scratch)}
At each iteration the agent collects a rollout of $T = 1024$ transitions, then
performs $K = 10$ epochs of minibatch SGD. Advantages are computed with GAE:
\begin{align}
\delta_t &= r_t + \gamma\, V(s_{t+1})\,(1 - d_{t+1}) - V(s_t), \\
A_t &= \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\,(1 - d_{t+1})\, A_{t+1}, \\
R_t &= A_t + V(s_t) \quad \text{(value targets)},
\end{align}
with $\gamma = 0.99$, $\lambda = 0.95$. I use the CleanRL-style convention in
which $d_{t+1}$ masks bootstrapping at episode boundaries (\code{compute\_gae}
in \code{agents/ppo.py}). The policy is optimised with the clipped surrogate
objective
\begin{equation}
L^{\mathrm{CLIP}} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \min\!\big( \rho_t \hat{A}_t,\;
\mathrm{clip}(\rho_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\, \hat{A}_t \big) \right],
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\rho_t = \exp\!\big( \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) - \log \pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t|s_t) \big), \quad \epsilon = 0.2,
\end{equation}
where advantages $\hat{A}_t$ are normalized \textbf{per minibatch}. The total
loss is
\begin{equation}
L = L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}_{\mathrm{pg}} - c_{\mathrm{ent}}\, H[\pi_\theta]
+ c_{\mathrm{vf}}\, \big( V_\theta(s_t) - R_t \big)^2,
\end{equation}
with entropy coefficient $c_{\mathrm{ent}} = 0.005$ (an exploration bonus; note
the \emph{negative} sign so entropy is \emph{maximised}) and value coefficient
$c_{\mathrm{vf}} = 0.5$. Gradients are clipped to a global norm of $0.5$, the
optimiser is Adam ($\text{lr} = 5\times10^{-4}$, $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$) and the
learning rate is \textbf{linearly decayed} to zero over training. The value
function is \emph{not} clipped---a deliberate, common simplification I note for
transparency.

\subsection{Double DQN (baseline)}
The off-policy baseline stores transitions in a replay buffer ($|B| = 50\,000$)
and minimises the temporal-difference error
\begin{align}
y &= r + \gamma\, Q_{\mathrm{target}}\!\big(s', \arg\max_a Q_{\mathrm{online}}(s', a)\big)(1 - d), \\
L &= \mathrm{MSE}\big( Q_{\mathrm{online}}(s, a),\, y \big),
\end{align}
i.e.\ the \textbf{Double DQN} target (online net selects the action, target net
evaluates it). The target network is hard-updated every $500$ gradient steps;
exploration uses $\epsilon$-greedy with $\epsilon$ decayed multiplicatively
($0.995$ per update) from $1.0$ to a floor of $0.05$; Adam uses
$\text{lr} = 10^{-4}$, batch size $256$, $\gamma = 0.99$. I deliberately keep
DQN as a \emph{simple, smaller baseline} (it uses the MLP Q-network only).

\subsection{Network Architectures}
All three networks consume the $5 \times 5$ observation.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{AttentionActorCritic.} A linear projection lifts each vehicle's 5
features to a $64$-d embedding; a \code{MultiheadAttention} layer ($4$ heads,
\code{batch\_first}) lets vehicles attend to one another; the result is
flattened ($5 \times 64 = 320$) and fed to separate two-hidden-layer
($64$--$64$, \code{tanh}) actor and critic heads.
\item \textbf{MlpActorCritic.} The observation is flattened ($25$-d) and passed
through a $25 \to 64 \to 320$ ReLU trunk, then the same $64$--$64$ \code{tanh}
actor/critic heads. The trunk width is chosen so the total parameter count
matches the attention model.
\item \textbf{MlpQNetwork (DQN).} A compact $25 \to 128 \to 128 \to 5$ ReLU
network.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Training.} $100\,000$ environment steps per run; PPO rollout $1024$;
common $\text{lr} = 5\times10^{-4}$. Core comparison configurations (all
\code{highway-v0}, aggressive style, \code{dur=80}): \textbf{PPO+Attention},
\textbf{PPO+MLP}, \textbf{DQN+MLP}. Each is trained with seeds $\{0, 1, 2\}$. I
seed Python, NumPy, PyTorch (with \code{cudnn.deterministic=True}) and the
environment reset.

\textbf{Reproducibility cross-check.} I additionally train \textbf{SB3 PPO}~\cite{sb3}
(\code{MlpPolicy}) with hyperparameters matched to our implementation
($\text{\code{n\_steps}}=1024$, $\text{batch}=256$, $\text{\code{n\_epochs}}=10$,
$\gamma=0.99$, $\lambda=0.95$, $\text{clip}=0.2$, $c_{\mathrm{ent}}=0.005$,
$c_{\mathrm{vf}}=0.5$, $\text{\code{max\_grad\_norm}}=0.5$,
$\text{lr}=5\times10^{-4}$), seed 0. Trend agreement validates our PPO algorithm
implementation (not the architecture, which differs).

\textbf{Evaluation protocol.} Because training returns under the same style are
nearly tied across methods (all three converge toward the same reward ceiling),
I evaluate \emph{behaviour} under a \textbf{common test configuration} (the
\code{base} style, \code{dur=80}, i.e.\ max 400 decision steps) so that all
policies---regardless of training style---are judged on the same distribution.
For each model I run \textbf{30 deterministic} (arg-max) episodes and report:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Crash rate} (\% of episodes ending in a collision)---\emph{lower
is safer};
\item \textbf{Average survival} (decision steps before episode end, max
400)---\emph{higher is better};
\item \textbf{Average speed} (m/s)---\emph{progress};
\item \textbf{Average lane changes} per episode---\emph{behavioural signature}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Hardware / software.} NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4050 Laptop GPU; PyTorch
\code{2.12.0+cu126}; Gymnasium + \code{highway-env}; Stable-Baselines3
\code{2.2.1}. Training runs were executed three-in-parallel; each $100$k-step
run takes on the order of 15--25~min wall-clock under this contention.

\textbf{PPO+Attention vs.\ PPO+MLP.} Both methods converge toward the same
ceiling ($266.7$, the approximate maximum achievable reward in this
configuration), but PPO+Attention arrives more reliably and earlier. The final
mean$\pm$std difference is substantial: $252.7\pm4.1$ vs.\ $203.1\pm21.4$. The
much larger standard deviation of PPO+MLP ($\pm21.4$) is the more consequential
finding: seed 0 of PPO+MLP reaches $224.5$ while seed 1 reaches only $181.8$,
suggesting the MLP policy is highly sensitive to the random initialisation---a
phenomenon consistent with the MLP feature extractor lacking the
permutation-equivariant inductive bias of attention and thereby requiring a
specific seed-dependent path through the non-convex loss landscape.
PPO+Attention, lacking this sensitivity, can be said to learn a more
consistently reachable solution.

\textbf{DQN vs.\ PPO.} Double DQN achieves a final rolling reward of
$237.5 \pm 5.7$ (seeds 0 \& 1), placing it \emph{between} the two PPO variants.
This is somewhat surprising given that DQN uses a notably smaller network
($20{,}485$ parameters vs.\ ${\sim}70{,}000$ for PPO), and may reflect the
benefit of the large replay buffer smoothing the notoriously high variance of
early learning in a collision-heavy environment. Critically, DQN reaches a
rolling-avg reward of $150$ at $\approx 7{,}700$ environment steps---%
\emph{earlier} than PPO's $10{,}240$---suggesting faster initial learning.
However, at the end of training PPO+Attention's final reward ($252.7$)
substantially exceeds DQN's ($237.5$). Direct comparison of ``steps-to-150''
should be interpreted cautiously: PPO's step counter advances in $1{,}024$-step
rollouts while DQN logs at episode boundaries; the effective agent-environment
interaction is counted the same way (total env steps) but the granularity of
the rolling average differs.

\subsection{Stable-Baselines3 cross-validation}
The SB3 PPO baseline (seed 0, identical hyperparameters) achieved a final
rolling-average reward of \textbf{261.2} and a peak of \textbf{266.7}, compared
to our custom PPO+Attention mean of $\mathbf{252.7 \pm 4.1}$ ($n=2$ seeds). The
SB3 value sits within one standard deviation of our two-seed estimate and
follows a nearly identical convergence trajectory (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}, orange
curve). This constitutes strong evidence that our from-scratch implementation
contains no systematic bugs: the performance gap between SB3 and our
implementation is smaller than the seed-to-seed variance of our own runs.

The SB3 architecture differs from ours (its \code{MlpPolicy} uses a
$[64, 64]$ shared trunk rather than our attention feature extractor), so the
comparison validates the \emph{algorithm} implementation, not the architecture.
Specifically, it confirms that our GAE, clipped surrogate objective, advantage
normalization, learning rate decay, and gradient clipping are all correctly
implemented.

\subsection{Reward-shaping / driving-style study}
Beyond the core ablation I trained PPO under the \emph{conservative} and
\emph{base} styles (seed 0) to illustrate how the \textbf{reward specification
dominates the qualitative behaviour}. Recall (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) that
absolute returns are not comparable across styles; the point of this study is
the \emph{behavioural} contrast, not the reward magnitude.

Table~\ref{tab:styles} reports the final training reward under each style for
seed 0 (PPO only; DQN was only trained under aggressive). The numbers appear to
show conservative ($\approx 380$--$400$) performing ``better'' than aggressive
($\approx 200$--$260$)---but this comparison is \emph{misleading} and must not
be made. The reward functions differ fundamentally: the conservative style
targets a low speed range ($[10, 20]$~m/s), reduces the collision penalty to
$-2.0$, and heavily penalises lane changes ($-0.5$), incentivising the agent to
drive slowly and smoothly with very few crashes and very few manoeuvres.
Because episodes seldom crash, the agent accumulates many small rewards over
the full 400-step horizon, producing high absolute return. The aggressive style
targets high speed ($[30, 40]$~m/s), heavily penalises crashes ($-4.0$), and
rewards lane changes ($+0.2$), incentivising fast, lane-changing behaviour. A
single crash wipes out many steps of reward, depressing the absolute return.

maximum speed, and occasionally collides. The reward-shaping specification is
the dominant determinant of qualitative driving style, confirming that reward
design---not algorithm choice---is the key lever for safety-critical
applications.

\section{Discussion}
\textbf{Does Self-Attention help?} Under these experimental conditions, the
answer is yes---but the more informative finding is the \emph{stability} effect:
PPO+Attention's variance across seeds ($\pm4.1$) is five times lower than
PPO+MLP's ($\pm21.4$). The mean improvement ($252.7$ vs.\ $203.1$) may or may
not generalise to more seeds or different environments; the stability
improvement is the more robust claim. I hypothesise that the attention
mechanism, by forming explicit soft-weights over which vehicle to attend to at
each decision step, provides a more consistent gradient signal across the
random-initialisation lottery than a fixed-topology MLP that must implicitly
discover the same structure.

\textbf{Why is DQN competitive with PPO+MLP?} I observe that Double DQN
($237.5 \pm 5.7$) outperforms PPO+MLP ($203.1 \pm 21.4$) on average under these
settings. This is not a universal property: DQN's off-policy replay buffer
smooths the reward variance of a collision-heavy early learning phase (where
$\epsilon$ is near $1.0$ and many episodes crash immediately), giving it more
stable early learning. However, I are cautious about this conclusion: the two
methods differ in more than just on/off-policy---network size ($20$k vs.\ $70$k),
optimiser schedule, and exploration mechanism are all different. This is why I
label DQN the \emph{baseline}, not a competing ablation arm.

\textbf{Sample efficiency.} All three methods reach the reward threshold
($150$) between $7{,}000$ and $10{,}000$ environment steps---a surprisingly
narrow window given the algorithmic differences. This suggests that for this
particular environment and reward scale, the difficulty is not in finding the
right direction (initial learning is fast for all methods) but in
\emph{reliably converging} to a high-reward policy. PPO+Attention achieves this
most consistently; PPO+MLP the least.

\textbf{Reward shaping dominates qualitative style.} The conservative /
aggressive style experiment underscores a practitioner lesson: no matter how
well the algorithm is tuned, it optimises exactly what the reward says. A policy
trained under the aggressive style drives faster and changes lanes more often
but crashes occasionally; a conservative policy is docile and safe but slow.
Neither is ``better''---they are responses to different objectives. For real
autonomous driving applications, reward engineering is the primary design lever;
algorithm selection is secondary.

\textbf{Implementation correctness.} The agreement between our custom PPO and
SB3 ($261.2$ vs.\ $252.7$, within one 2-seed standard deviation) gives us
reasonable confidence that the GAE, clipped surrogate, advantage normalisation,
and linear learning-rate decay are all correctly implemented. The residual gap
is most plausibly explained by architectural differences (SB3's $[64,64]$ trunk
vs.\ our attention model) rather than bugs, given the SB3 result actually
\emph{above} our mean.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Few seeds.} Three seeds bound variance only coarsely; firm claims
of superiority would need $\geq 5$ seeds and a significance test. I therefore
phrase comparative results as ``observed under these conditions''.
\item \textbf{Single primary environment.} The core ablation is on
\code{highway-v0}. I provide multi-environment \emph{support} (\code{merge},
\code{roundabout}, \code{intersection}) but do not claim generalization of the
ablation result across all of them.
\item \textbf{Compute-bounded budget.} $100$k steps is modest; some
configurations may not be fully converged. Hyperparameters were matched, not
individually tuned, which can favour or disfavour either architecture.
\item \textbf{Evaluation determinism.} I evaluate the arg-max policy; a
stochastic-policy evaluation could shift absolute numbers (though not, I
expect, the ranking).
\item \textbf{DQN is intentionally a weak baseline} (smaller net, aggressive
$\epsilon$-decay, no attention variant); it bounds, rather than maximises,
off-policy performance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
I presented a reproduction and ablation study of PPO and Double DQN on the
discrete-action \code{highway-env} driving simulator. Our from-scratch PPO
implementation---with GAE, clipped surrogate objective, per-minibatch advantage
normalisation, linear learning-rate decay, and gradient clipping---was verified
against Stable-Baselines3 (final reward $252.7 \pm 4.1$ vs.\ SB3's $261.2$),
confirming correctness. In a parameter-matched architectural ablation (attention
$66{,}822$ params vs.\ MLP $72{,}262$ params, a $7.5\%$ gap), PPO with
Self-Attention achieved a $24.5\%$ higher final reward than PPO with MLP under
aggressive driving style ($252.7$ vs.\ $203.1$), and, more strikingly, a
five-fold reduction in cross-seed variance ($\pm4.1$ vs.\ $\pm21.4$). I
attribute this to the permutation-equivariant inductive bias of the attention
mechanism providing more consistent gradients across random initialisations. A
reward-shaping study confirmed that driving style is determined primarily by the
reward specification, not the algorithm.

\textbf{Future work} could expand this to $\geq 5$ seeds for more reliable
significance estimates, test the same ablation on \code{merge-v0} and
\code{roundabout-v0} for environment generalisation, and compare to a
prioritised-experience-replay DQN variant to give the off-policy baseline a
fairer chance. The modular code structure released alongside this paper makes
these extensions straightforward.
